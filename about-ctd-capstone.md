# About the CTD Senior Capstone at CU Boulder

A general reference on how the Creative Technology and Design (CTD) senior capstone at the University of Colorado Boulder works: the two courses, the fall research semester, the assignments and deliverables, how proposals are judged, the spring build semester, ATLAS Expo, and past projects. It contains no information about any specific student's project.

Compiled October 2026 from the CU Boulder course catalog, ATLAS Institute web pages, and the Fall 2026 ATLS 4000 assignments. Assignment names, dates, and details can vary by instructor and year. Where something was not confirmed, it says so.

---

## 1. The program

- **Degree:** BS in Creative Technology and Design (CTD), an interdisciplinary engineering degree from the ATLAS Institute, conferred by the College of Engineering and Applied Science at CU Boulder.
- **What CTD covers:** computing, digital and physical design, and art. Students spend much of their time prototyping. Focus areas include physical computing, product design, fabrication, HCI and UX/UI, games, AR/VR, immersive experiences, installations, robotics, and software.
- **Where the capstone fits:** freshman and sophomore years cover engineering and CTD core courses. Junior year adds focus electives. Senior year is the **two-semester capstone sequence** plus remaining electives. The program describes it as applying "everything you've learned in a year-long Senior Capstone project."
- **Home base:** the Roser ATLAS Center on the CU Boulder campus. Its BTU Lab is open to students and has laser cutters, 3D printers, a woodshop, a CNC, and an electronics bench.

## 2. The two courses

| | Fall: ATLS 4000 | Spring: ATLS 4010 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title | Research Methods and Professional Practice (RMPP) | Capstone Projects |
| Credits | 3 | 4 (repeatable up to 8 total) |
| Purpose | Find, research, and validate a project | Build, iterate on, document, and exhibit it |
| Ends with | A pitch presentation and a written Proposal Document | Final presentation, ATLAS Expo, and project documentation |
| Prerequisites | ATLS 3100 and ATLS 3300 (C- or better); CTD majors only | ATLS 4000 (C- or better); CTD majors only |

**ATLS 4000 catalog description (verbatim):** "Research Methods and Professional Practice lays the foundation for students to produce culminating work in their major. In this course, students will engage in domain exploration, learn to define a research problem, utilize tools and methods for professional design research, refine presentation skills, and engage in iterative and collaborative work. This course is focused on domain research, idea validation, and prototyping in order to prepare students for a culminating semester-long Capstone project based on this research."

**ATLS 4010 catalog description (verbatim):** "Focuses on the development of an individual thesis project. Specific class sessions will feature a combination of lectures, demonstrations, guest speakers, lab sessions, and critiques. This course also entails group work, portfolio development, critical theoretical readings, and a significant written component."

ATLAS also describes the capstone as a studio course with critique and presentation sessions that include internal and external guest critics. At the end, students publish a fully articulated project description and external documentation.

## 3. Fall semester (ATLS 4000 / RMPP): the arc

The fall semester moves from "who am I and what do I care about" to "here is a validated, scoped project I can build next semester." In Fall 2026 the sequence looked roughly like this. Names and exact dates vary.

1. **Introduce yourself** (week 2 or 3)
2. **Goals and priorities** (early September)
3. **Module 2, Narrowing:** ideation jam, inspiration log, and choosing a faculty sounding board (September)
4. **Sounding board meeting** with a faculty member (late September)
5. **Draft pitch presentation** and written reflection (early October)
6. **Capstone commitment form:** confirm your group and roles (October)
7. **Feasibility tests:** plan and run technical and audience tests (October and November)
8. **Final pitch presentation and Proposal Document** (end of semester)

Throughout, students are expected to do three kinds of validation (precedent research, user research, and feasibility tests), because the final proposal is graded on them.

## 4. Fall assignments in detail

### 4.1 Introductory slides
- 2 to 3 Google Slides (other formats not accepted), with your name on every slide.
- Content:
  - a little about yourself
  - topics, problems, or audiences you're interested in
  - your current skills
  - your goals for capstone (a concrete idea only briefly, if you have one)
  - optionally, complementary skills you're looking for in groupmates
- Presented in class, **3 minutes maximum**, strictly enforced. Slides are copied at a set deadline.

### 4.2 Capstone Goals and Priorities
A brainstorming scaffold with a **minimum of 5 entries per category**:
- **Skills I already have:** technical, interpersonal, or soft skills, with where you learned or demonstrated each.
- **Skills I'm keen to develop:** why, and how you plan to develop them.
- **Topic/problem and target audience:** problems you care about, and who has them.
- **Technologies and media:** for example mobile apps, wearables, physical computing, AR/VR, machine learning, installation art, board games, haptics.
- **Capstone goals:** 3 to 5 specific goals for yourself.
- **Synthesis and priorities:** highlight the 3 to 5 items that matter most to you.

The guiding question: "What do you hope to accomplish, both for yourself and for others?"

### 4.3 Module 2, Narrowing: Ideation Jam and Inspiration Log
Three parts:
1. **Venn diagrams and ideas (done partly in groups in class).** Each Venn diagram crosses a **topic**, a **technology or medium**, and an **audience**. Students make several diagrams and list a **minimum of 5 ideas** per diagram.
2. **Inspiration log.** Choose one favorite idea or diagram and find **3 to 6 pieces of inspiration or precedent**. Each needs an image, title, description, link or source, and 1 to 2 sentences on what you like or dislike about it.
3. **Sounding board pre-assignment.** Name the faculty member you'd like feedback from (ATLAS preferred, not required).

Later class sessions build on this:
- **Patterns in precedents:** what's similar to and different from your idea.
- **User journeys:** a concise, clear story of a user going through the experience, for use in the draft pitch.

### 4.4 Sounding board
A one-on-one or small-group meeting with a faculty member to get early feedback on the idea. The written submission covers:
1. who you met with and when
2. what you discussed and what feedback you got
3. your next steps based on that feedback
4. a copy of your meeting notes

### 4.5 Draft pitch presentation and reflection
- A short in-class pitch of the current idea (in Fall 2026, roughly 4 to 5 minutes) with faculty and peer feedback.
- **The written reflection covers:**
  - the feedback you received and how you'll respond to it
  - what you learned from peers' presentations
  - your idea and group commitment
- Students noted that feedback can pull in different directions. Some pushes toward technical difficulty and heavy CAD or fabrication, some toward usefulness. Delivery matters too: presenters who tell a clear story get better feedback.

### 4.6 Capstone Commitment Form
A Google Form confirming who you're working with. Fields:
- full name
- an elevator pitch with a link to slides
- keywords (for example music, fabrication, education, graphic design, app development, game development, art installation)
- group members
- an external client or organization, if any, with a point person and email
- whether you still want help finding groupmates
- anything else the instructors should know

**Group role rule:** groups must give each person **specific roles** showing how they'll contribute meaningfully, **3 credits' worth**, over the spring semester. The form's example shows the expected level of detail, with roles like Lead Game Designer, Lead Programmer, Art Director, Sound Designer, and UX Researcher/Producer, each with a paragraph of concrete responsibilities. Scope is expected to grow with group size: faculty may say an idea is too small for the number of people.

### 4.7 Feasibility tests
Each student or group runs experiments connecting the idea to the technologies and methods needed to build it. The goal is to find out what will bring the project to life, or show that something won't.
- **Submission:** a short Google Doc or text entry with (1) names and a short project description, and (2) a list of tests with deadlines and owners. For example: "Test 1: Storyboarding, done by 10/23 at 5pm (name)."
- **Guiding rule:** **"Do the tests you're most afraid of first."**
- **Required test types:**
  - **Technical tests.** Test every technology the project relies on. If it uses an API, pull from it. If it uses Unity, build a small proof of concept. If it uses physical computing, build a working circuit and code with the actual sensors and motors. Bottom line: prove you can use the tech you're proposing.
  - **Interview and audience tests.** Does the problem or experience exist? What are people dealing with now, and what do they currently do about it? These can be interviews, surveys, or formal or informal observation. For artworks that don't answer a specific problem, meet with outside experts to discuss the larger questions and the experience you're designing.
  - **Full project renderings.** Required for all proposals: for example a 3D model of an installation, an illustration of a product, or an animatic or still from a scene. "Show us, don't tell us."

## 5. The final fall deliverables and how they're graded

From the Capstone Project Proposal Rubric (Research Methods and Professional Practice).

**Deliverables:**
1. **A presentation** that clearly states a proposed project aimed at a specific audience, with a problem or experience you're designing around. It must state the project's **Domains of Interest**: the subject matter, and the tools or platforms you'll use. Presentations happen at the end of the semester.
2. **A completed Proposal Document**, realistic and challenging within the capstone timeframe. It must include:
   - a **detailed production timeline**
   - a **bill of materials and/or list of functional specs**
   - **visual renderings** of the work

**ATLAS faculty judge proposals on four things:** Research, Feasibility, Preparedness, and Relevance to CTD.

**Rubric for a successful proposal and pitch:**
- It improves the world in some way, or adds value to an experience, through engineering, art and/or design.
- It shows technical mastery specific to engineering/CTD in the process, execution, and/or presentation.
- It's designed for a clearly defined experience and/or problem, focused on a specific audience.
- The student has completed **three stages of idea validation:**
  1. **Precedent research:** thorough research into what's already been done in the field, successfully and unsuccessfully. Clearly written up in the proposal and briefly explained in the pitch.
  2. **User interviews and observations:** connecting with users or the audience, mentors, and experts to explore the problem or experience and test possible solutions. This answers: does the problem actually exist, and is the proposed solution appropriate and effective?
  3. **Feasibility tests:** a series of small experiments testing the content and technical feasibility. This answers: is it technically feasible, and is the scope right for capstone?
- The project is feasible while still challenging the students' current technical skills.
- The proposal document is detailed and complete, including a full timeline and a bill of materials or functional specs.

## 6. Spring semester (ATLS 4010) and ATLAS Expo

- **What happens:** students build the project proposed in the fall. Class sessions mix lectures, demos, guest speakers, lab time, and critiques with internal and external critics. The course also includes group work, portfolio development, critical theory readings, and a significant written component.
- **End of semester:** students present their finished project publicly. In past years each student or group presented for **no more than 5 minutes**, followed by commentary from a panel of reviewers.
- **Documentation:** students publish a full project description and external documentation.
- **ATLAS Expo:** the ATLAS Institute's largest public event, in the Roser ATLAS Center, showing work from across the ATLAS community, including CTD capstones, thesis projects, and outstanding coursework. Interactive and tangible work is especially encouraged.
  - Expo 2025 was Thursday, May 1, 2025, 4:00 to 6:30 pm.
  - Expo 2026 was Friday, April 24, 2026, 3:30 to 6:00 pm, with 100+ projects across four floors.
  - The Expo 2027 date was not announced as of October 2026; late April is likely.

### Who reviews final presentations
Past panels mixed academics and industry. In 2022, reviewers included:
- an experimental game designer and instructor
- a game producer and studio founder
- a sculptor turned metal fabricator turned software engineer
- a CTD graduate in an electrical engineering master's program
- a physicist working on prosthetics and bioplastics
- a senior creative technologist at a digital agency
- an ATLAS PhD student in tangible interaction
- a startup investor and entrepreneur-in-residence
- a medical device consultant

So expect questions from both sides: "who is this for and would they use or buy it" and "how did you build it."

## 7. Research ethics note

CU Boulder's Institutional Review Board (IRB) guidance says not every class activity counts as human-subjects research. If a project does meet the definition, an undergraduate cannot be the principal investigator; a faculty member must be. Exempt status is an IRB decision, and interactive exempt research still needs an informed-consent process. For surveys and interviews, ask the instructor whether an IRB determination is needed. Either way:
- tell participants what the research is for, and that it's optional
- collect only what you need
- keep personal contact information private

## 8. Past capstone projects

### Spring 2022 (presented Sunday, May 1, 2022, in four sessions)

**Session 1: games and interactive stories**
- **Oasis:** an immersive nature video game that draws players in with graphics, narrative, and sensory design, then pushes them away with dull gameplay, as a critique of using technology to escape reality.
- **Ludo:** an online arcade of accessible games and tutorials for non-gamers.
- **The Gremlinizer:** an online social word game.
- **Zombies of London:** a competitive board game set in a post-apocalyptic London.
- **Queerland, TX:** a competitive strategy board game about taking over a nightclub scene.
- **Carbon Zero Home Design:** a game that teaches lower-carbon living through designing and remodeling a home.
- **The Primrose Garden Path:** a UI-based game about sinister corporate forces and office mundanity.
- **Ward7:** interactive storytelling about disability and magic.

**Session 2: installations, fashion, media**
- **REWEAR:** a collection imagining a transparent, sustainable future for festival fashion.
- **Merkaba Meditation:** an interactive structure using color and sound to deepen meditation.
- **Mycorrhizal:** an interactive installation about human connection, using fungal networks as a metaphor.
- **V0ID P0RTAL:** an installation where a button press sends you through surreal portals.
- **The Black Spot in the Basement:** a 2-minute animated short about mental health and depression.
- **Unheard Voices:** an original electronic music set with live instruments, raising awareness and money for youth homelessness.
- **CALLOSUM:** an interactive installation and workbook teaching the brain science of musical taste.
- **GNFT:** a web app for creating and minting generative-art NFTs from a P5.js playground.

**Session 3: apps, web, and products**
- **FemmeFit:** a mobile app for women starting strength training, with plans, community, and gym finding.
- **Open3D Car Parts:** open-source 3D-printable replacement interior parts for older vehicles.
- **Ecolyze:** a habit-tracking app that quantifies carbon saved.
- **Design Patterns:** an iOS app teaching software design patterns.
- **Beholder Nexus:** a hub for alternative-controller game developers and players (React, Node, Docker, SQL).
- **Pet Home Alone:** the UX for an app that alerts someone when a pet needs care.
- **Make Your Mark:** an auto-destructive clock artwork about time spent online vs. in reality.

**Session 4: engineering, health, and education**
- **Gait-Keeper:** a sensing knee brace that alerts the wearer to abnormal gait and shares data with a physical therapist.
- **SimpleServo:** an open-source PCB that lets students control servos without programming.
- **Teeth to Teach:** 3D-modeled teeth for dental students, for haptic simulators or 3D printing.
- **Autonomous Perception and Exploration:** research projects on robot perception and mapping, including a point-cloud publication and an autonomous rover.
- **Here Comes Ralphie:** a published children's book with a motion-design animation about integrity and resilience.
- **Willie the Wolf:** a pop-up children's book with interactive paper circuits about cyberbullying.

**Patterns across past projects:**
- **Mediums:** they range from games, apps, and installations to physical products, wearables, PCBs, books, and research.
- **Group size:** solo projects and groups of up to three were both common.
- **Physical products:** were the minority, for example the sensing knee brace, printable car parts, and teeth models.
- **Strongest projects:** paired a clear audience and problem with a working, demonstrable build.

## 9. Practical tips that come up repeatedly

- **Start from a real problem or experience, not a technology.** The rubric's first question is whether it improves the world or adds value to an experience.
- **Validate early, and in this order:** precedents, then people (interviews, surveys, observation), then small feasibility tests. Do the scariest test first.
- **Scope to the team:** each member needs a semester of real work, and the project must be finishable in one build semester.
- **Plan the proposal around its required parts:** timeline, bill of materials or functional specs, and renderings.
- **Present the work as a story:** problem, audience, what exists, what you'll build, how you know it can work, and what's next. Clear storytelling gets better feedback.
- **Document as you go:** spring requires portfolio material and a written component, and the Expo needs a demo-ready build.
